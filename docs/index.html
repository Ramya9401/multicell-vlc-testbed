Two lamps, one floor, three ways to share it

A software-defined visible-light testbed, and what happens to a user who walks from directly under one LED to the dead zone between two.

Ramya Mummadi, Xinyu Zhang, Alexis A. Dowhuszko, Konstantinos G. Rallis, Risto Wichman, Jyri Hämäläinen
15th Int. Symposium on Communication Systems, Networks and Digital Signal Processing · DOI 10.1109/CSNDSP68462.2026.11654482

GNU Radio + USRP-N210 OFDM / IM-DD ZF precoding Joint transmission

Indoor lighting is already a dense, overlapping grid of emitters. If you modulate those LEDs fast enough to carry data, you inherit that grid as your cell plan — and you inherit its worst property: every luminaire spills into its neighbour's cell. This paper builds the link on real hardware and measures what that spill costs, then fixes it two different ways.

The headline result is a map rather than a number. In a two-cell setup with the LEDs 170 cm apart, no single transmission scheme wins everywhere. Plain reuse is best for about 22% of the floor, zero-forcing precoding for 46%, and joint transmission for the remaining 32%. The page below walks the signal chain that produces that split, with each stage runnable.

About the simulations. Every panel on this page is a physical model built from the equations in the paper, calibrated to the measured geometry (1.6 m mounting height, 1.7 m LED separation). They are there to show the mechanism and let you push it. The measured results are reported separately, as measurements, in the tables.
01

The channel is a cosine lobe

A phosphor-converted white LED radiates as a Lambertian emitter: brightness falls off as a power of the cosine of the angle off boresight. Put a photodetector on the floor and the channel gain between them is pure geometry.

(2)H = (m+1)·A / (2π·d²) · cosm(φ) · Ts(ψ) · g(ψ) · cos(ψ),   0 ≤ ψ ≤ Ψc with Lambertian order m = −ln2 / ln(cos Φ½). Here A is the detector's active area, d the LED-to-PD distance, φ the irradiance angle, ψ the incidence angle, Ts the optical filter gain and g the concentrator gain. Outside the field of view Ψc, the gain is zero — a hard edge, not a roll-off.

Two things in that expression do the damage. The 1/d² term is ordinary free-space spreading. The cosm(φ) term is sharper: with the detector flat on the floor and the LED pointing straight down, both angles collapse to the same one, and the gain goes as hm+1 / dm+3. A narrow-beam LED (large m) therefore builds a tight, bright cell with a steep skirt — good for isolation, bad for coverage.

The paper validates this against measurement in both planes. Vertically, the Lambertian model tracks the measured gain closely across the whole range. Horizontally, it tracks to about 70 cm from the LED, then the measurement starts to run above the model — wall reflections and ambient light contribute power that a pure line-of-sight model does not predict.

Push Φ½ down and watch the lobe narrow: peak gain climbs, but the usable radius collapses. Push the field of view down and the curve acquires vertical walls — past Ψc the detector simply stops seeing the lamp. That cliff is why VLC cell planning is a lighting problem as much as a radio one.

02

Getting bits onto light

The modulation is intensity modulation with direct detection: the LED's brightness carries the waveform, and the photodetector responds to received optical power. There is no phase to recover and no negative light. The received signal is

(1)r(t) = R · h · x(t) + n(t) where R is the detector responsivity, h the channel gain from (2), and n(t) the combined shot and thermal noise of the photodetector.

OFDM is attractive here for the usual reason — it turns a frequency-selective channel into a stack of flat subcarriers — but it generates a complex, bipolar signal, and light cannot be negative. Optical OFDM normally solves this with Hermitian symmetry, spending half the spectrum to force a real-valued output. This testbed takes the other route: a passband scheme, upconverting the complex baseband onto an intermediate frequency so the time-domain waveform comes out real without the symmetry constraint. A DC bias then lifts the whole thing above zero so it can drive the LED.

That bias is the central tension of the design. Too little and the waveform's negative excursions clip at zero. Too much and the LED leaves its linear region at the top, and you are burning optical power on a constant term that carries no information. OFDM's high peak-to-average power ratio makes the window tight.

Set 16-QAM and drop the SNR below about 16 dB: the constellation clouds merge and the error count runs away. That sensitivity is exactly why the measured 16-QAM coverage radius in the paper is so much tighter than QPSK's. Separately, raise the drive amplitude past the bias headroom and the clipping counter rises — clipping noise lands back in-band and the constellation smears even with the noise slider at its cleanest.

03

What one cell can actually deliver

With a single LED and a single detector, the link SNR follows straight from the channel gain:

(3)SNRSISO = (G · R · Ptx · h)² / σn² with G the transimpedance amplifier gain, Ptx the transmitted optical power, and σn² the total noise variance at the detector.

From there the bit error rate is textbook AWGN, which is the point: it gives a theoretical curve to measure against.

(4)BERQPSK = ½ · erfc( √(Eb/N₀) )
(5)BERM-QAM ≈ 3/(2(M−1)) · erfc( √( 3/(M−1) · Eb/N₀ ) )

Measured against those curves, QPSK tracks theory across most of the cell and falls below 10⁻⁶ close in. 16-QAM holds only inside about 90 cm; beyond that its BER climbs to the order of 10⁻¹ — unusable. The binding constraint is not the optics but the phosphor: the yellow phosphor that converts blue LED emission to white has a slow response, which caps the electrical modulation bandwidth and sets the single-cell coverage radius at 150–160 cm.

04

Add a second lamp, lose the cell edge

Now mount a second identical LED 170 cm away and let it serve its own user on the same resources. Frequency reuse doubles the aggregate rate, and for a user sitting under either lamp it costs almost nothing. For a user in the overlap it is close to fatal: both signals arrive at comparable strength, so the second one is not a weak nuisance but a co-equal competitor.

(9)SINRno-ZF = (G·R·Ptx,1·H1,1)² / [ (G·R·Ptx,2·H2,1)² + σn² ]

Both fixes keep the receiver dumb — all the intelligence sits at the transmitter, which matters because a VLC receiver is a cheap photodiode and you do not want successive interference cancellation running on it.

Zero-forcing: cancel the interference

With channel state information at the transmitter, precode the two streams so that what lands at each detector is only its own data. The precoder is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the channel matrix:

(8)WZF = HT(H·HT)−1   ⟹   Heff = H·W = I₂

Columns are then normalised so each LED stays inside its permitted intensity range — this is a lighting fixture, and it has a brightness it is supposed to maintain. Zero-forcing has a well-known cost: when the two channel rows become similar, the inverse blows up, the precoder amplifies, and after normalisation the useful signal shrinks. That is noise enhancement, and in this geometry it is worst precisely at the midpoint where the two gains are equal.

Joint transmission: use the interference

The alternative inverts the premise. Instead of separating the users, send the same stream from both LEDs and let the optical powers add constructively at the detector:

(11)SNRJT = (G·R·Ptx,1·H1,1 + G·R·Ptx,2·H2,1)² / σn²

At the midpoint, where each lamp alone is at its weakest, two equal contributions add to roughly four times the power. The interference becomes signal. The price is that joint transmission is single-user — both lamps are busy serving one person, so the others have to be scheduled orthogonally in time.

Drag the position slider from 0 to 170 cm and the three curves trade places in a specific order. Near the lamp, plain reuse is fine — the interferer is 20 dB down and precoding only costs you power. Through the inner cell edge, no-precoding collapses while zero-forcing holds a usable level. Approaching the midpoint, the channel matrix turns ill-conditioned, the precoder's norm explodes, and zero-forcing falls off too — while joint transmission, which has been the flattest curve all along, comes out on top.

Raise the CSI error slider and watch zero-forcing degrade from both ends. Perfect cancellation is a modelling assumption; residual cross-talk is what the hardware actually delivers.

05

The floor divides into three

Converting those curves to achievable spectral efficiency and taking the best scheme at each position partitions the service area. This is the paper's real conclusion: not that one scheme wins, but that the floor is zoned, and a practical multi-cell VLC system has to know which zone a user is standing in.

Which scheme “wins” depends on how you count. Compared link by link, joint transmission wins everywhere by construction — two lamps pushing one stream can never be worse than one. The comparison that means something counts throughput per cell: reuse and zero-forcing serve two users at once, while joint transmission serves one and the others wait their turn. With the testbed geometry and a 12% CSI error, the model partitions the floor roughly 25 / 44 / 32 against the measured 22 / 46 / 32 — close enough to say the mechanism is the one the hardware is showing, far enough to remember these are different things. Move any slider and the boundaries move with it. The CSI error slider is the most revealing: at 1% zero-forcing holds almost the whole floor, and by 20% it has surrendered most of the outer edge to joint transmission. How well you know the channel decides how much of the room you can cancel interference in.

The reuse-preference margin is the other honest knob. Plain reuse needs no CSI, no coordination and no scheduler, so a deployment would reasonably keep using it while it is merely close to optimal rather than strictly best. Widening that margin grows the grey band outward from each lamp.

07

What it adds up to

  • The phosphor sets the ceiling. Coverage radius lands at 150–160 cm not because of optics or noise but because the phosphor's slow conversion caps modulation bandwidth. Blue filtering or micro-LEDs are the way past it.
  • Transmitter-side linear precoding is enough. No successive interference cancellation, no NOMA at the receiver. A photodiode and an FFT, with the complexity pushed to the lighting controller, which is mains-powered and already networked.
  • Zero-forcing owns the inner edge, joint transmission the outer. They fail in opposite directions: ZF to noise enhancement as the channel matrix degenerates, JT to its single-user scheduling cost when the overlap is weak.
  • Position is the scheduling variable. The practical system needs coarse localisation to pick a scheme — and VLC gives that nearly for free, since channel gain is a direct function of geometry.

The open question the split invites is the handover: the boundaries move with mounting height, separation, and detector field of view, so a deployment would need to learn its own zones rather than inherit these. The region map in panel 05 is the tool for that, and it is also where the model stops and the next experiment starts.
