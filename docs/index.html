I. Introduction

Indoor lighting is already a dense, overlapping grid of emitters. Modulating those light-emitting diodes (LEDs) fast enough to carry data means inheriting that grid as a cell plan, and inheriting its worst property: every luminaire spills into its neighbour's cell. Uniform illumination requires densely packed luminaires, so coverage areas overlap by design. That overlap is desirable for lighting and destructive for communication, because it produces strong inter-cell interference precisely where a user is furthest from any serving lamp.

Coordinated Multi-Point (CoMP) transmission addresses the same problem in radio-frequency networks by synchronising the transmission of neighbouring base stations, either to reinforce the desired signal or to cancel the interfering one. The paper applies that idea to a two-cell visible light communication (VLC) system and measures what it is worth, using a software-defined testbed built from GNU Radio, USRP hardware, phosphor-converted white LEDs and low-cost black-silicon photodetectors.

Two linear transmitter-side schemes are considered: Zero-Forcing (ZF) precoding, which separates two users in the spatial domain, and Joint Transmission (JT), which uses both lamps to serve one user. Both keep the receiver trivial, which matters because a VLC receiver is a photodiode and a transimpedance amplifier, not a platform for successive interference cancellation.

The headline result is a map rather than a number. With the LEDs 170 cm apart, no single scheme wins everywhere: plain reuse is best for roughly 22% of the service area, ZF precoding for 46%, and JT for the remaining 32%. The sections below follow the signal chain that produces that split, with each stage runnable.

II. System Model for a Single-Cell VLC System

At the transmitter, a data stream is encoded with a header and a cyclic redundancy check, mapped onto QAM symbols across OFDM subcarriers, and converted to an intensity-modulated optical signal. At the receiver, a photodetector performs direct detection and the recovered waveform is synchronised, demodulated, equalised and decoded. The received signal in continuous time is

r(t) = R h x(t) + n(t),(1)

where R is the responsivity of the photodetector, x(t) the transmitted optical signal, h the single-input single-output (SISO) VLC channel gain, and n(t) the combined shot and thermal noise.

A. Lambertian channel gain

The channel gain depends on the position of the transmitter-receiver pair and on the radiation pattern of the LED, modelled as a Lambertian emitter. Accounting for the field-of-view angle Ψc of the photodetector,

H = (m+1)A⁄2πd² · cosm(φ) Ts(ψ) g(ψ) cos(ψ),0 ≤ ψ ≤ Ψc, and H = 0 otherwise,(2)

where m = −ln2 ⁄ ln(cos Φ1/2) is the Lambertian order, Φ1/2 the half-intensity angle, A the active area of the detector, d the transmitter-receiver distance, φ and ψ the irradiance and incidence angles, and Ts and g the optical filter and concentrator gains.

Two terms in (2) do the damage. The 1⁄d² factor is ordinary free-space spreading. The cosm(φ) factor is sharper: with the LED pointing straight down and the detector flat on the floor, both angles collapse to the same one, and the gain falls as hm+1⁄dm+3. A narrow-beam LED therefore builds a tight, bright cell with a steep skirt, which is good for isolation and bad for coverage. The field of view contributes a hard edge rather than a roll-off: past Ψc the detector stops seeing the lamp entirely.

The model was validated against measurement in both planes. Vertically, the measured gain follows the theoretical prediction closely across the evaluated range. Horizontally, it follows to approximately 70 cm from the LED, beyond which the measurement runs above the model. The deviation is attributed to non-ideal LED radiation patterns, wall reflections and ambient illumination, none of which a pure line-of-sight Lambertian model captures.

III. OFDM Waveform and Optical Front End

The modulation is intensity modulation with direct detection (IM/DD): LED brightness carries the waveform and the detector responds to received optical power. There is no phase to recover, and no negative light.

OFDM is attractive for the usual reason, in that it turns a frequency-selective channel into a stack of flat subcarriers, but it produces a complex bipolar signal. Optical OFDM normally resolves this with Hermitian symmetry, spending half the spectrum to force a real-valued output. The testbed instead adopts a passband approach: the complex baseband signal is upconverted onto an intermediate frequency, producing a real-valued time-domain waveform without the symmetry constraint. A DC bias then lifts the waveform above zero so that it can drive the LED.

That bias is the central tension of the optical front end. Too little, and the negative excursions clip at zero. Too much, and the LED leaves its linear region at the top while optical power is spent on a constant term carrying no information. The high peak-to-average power ratio of OFDM makes the usable window narrow, which is why the testbed drives the LED through a high-power amplifier held in its linear region.

IV. Single-Cell Link Performance

For a single LED and a single detector, the received SNR follows directly from the channel gain,

SNRSISO = (G R Ptx h)² ⁄ σn²,(3)

where G is the transimpedance amplifier gain, Ptx the transmitted optical power and σn² the total noise variance at the detector. Deriving Eb⁄N0 from (3), the theoretical bit error rate for QPSK is

BERQPSK = ½ erfc( √( Eb⁄N0 ) ),(4)

and for M-QAM over an additive white Gaussian noise channel,

BERM-QAM ≈ 3⁄2(M−1) erfc( √( 3⁄M−1 · Eb⁄N0 ) ).(5)

Measured against these curves, QPSK follows theory closely at intermediate distances and falls below 1×10−6 at short range. 16-QAM remains usable only inside approximately 90 cm; beyond that its BER rises sharply to the order of 1×10−1. QPSK therefore holds across most of the cell while 16-QAM works only close to the lamp.

The binding constraint is not the optics and not the noise. The yellow phosphor that converts blue LED emission to white light responds slowly, which caps the electrical modulation bandwidth and fixes the single-cell coverage radius at 150–160 cm.

V. Multi-User Transmission Schemes

The setup is extended to two spatially separated LEDs serving two users, one per cell, within partially overlapping coverage areas. Reusing the same resources in both cells improves aggregate throughput but introduces inter-cell interference. For a user beneath either lamp the interferer is weak; for a user in the overlap it arrives at comparable strength and is not a nuisance but a co-equal competitor. Without precoding,

SINRno-ZF = (G R Ptx,1H1,1)² ⁄ [ (G R Ptx,2H2,1)² + σn² ],(6)

where H1,1 is the desired gain from the serving LED and H2,1 the interfering gain from the adjacent one.

A. Zero-forcing precoding

With channel state information (CSI) at the transmitter, the received signal vector in the multi-user MIMO system is y = HWx + n. Zero-forcing designs W so that the effective channel becomes the identity, using the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse,

WZF = HT(HHT)−1,   Heff = HW = I2.(7)

Column-wise normalisation is then applied so that each LED stays within its allowable intensity range; this is a lighting fixture, and it has a brightness it is expected to maintain. The resulting SINR is

SINRZF = |G R Ptx,1Heff1,1|² ⁄ [ |G R Ptx,2Heff2,1|² + σn² ].(8)

Ideally the off-diagonal terms vanish. In practice there is always residual cross-talk, caused by CSI estimation error among other non-idealities. Zero-forcing also carries a structural cost: as the two channel rows become similar the matrix approaches singularity, the precoder norm grows, and after normalisation the useful signal shrinks. In this geometry that noise enhancement is worst precisely at the midpoint, where the two gains are equal.

B. Joint transmission

The alternative inverts the premise. Implemented as a single-user MISO scheme, both LEDs transmit the same stream and the received optical signals combine constructively:

SNRJT = (G R Ptx,1H1,1 + G R Ptx,2H2,1)² ⁄ σn².(9)

At the midpoint, where each lamp alone is weakest, two equal contributions add to roughly four times the power: the interference becomes signal. The cost is that JT is single-user, so the remaining users must be scheduled orthogonally, for example by time-division multiple access.

VI. Experimental Results and Data Analysis

Measurements were taken on a software-defined architecture combining GNU Radio, USRP-N210 hardware and an optical front end, summarised in Fig. 7 and Table II. The two-cell configuration uses two identical LEDs separated by 170 cm and mounted at a height of 160 cm, with the detector moved horizontally along the line between them to emulate user mobility.

A. Measured SINR against position

For both QPSK and 16-QAM the same spatial trend appears. With the detector within 0–30 cm of its serving LED, SINR is high: the desired signal is strong and the interferer weak. By approximately 40 cm, interference becomes noticeable and SINR begins to decline. The degradation becomes severe in the 60–80 cm overlap, where the two signals are of similar strength and the measured no-precoding SINR approaches very low values near the midpoint.

Applying ZF precoding produces a clear improvement across the interference-dominant region, with SINR remaining significantly higher between 40 and 70 cm. The gain is larger for 16-QAM, which is the more interference-sensitive of the two. Beyond 70–80 cm the benefit recedes, as residual cross-talk from non-ideal CSI and the noise-enhancement effect of ZF come to dominate.

Under JT the picture changes qualitatively. Across the full 0–170 cm range the SNR curve is nearly flat. At the central position near 85 cm, where both individual links reach their minimum, the joint transmission SNR is substantially higher than either single-cell case, confirming that coordinated transmission converts co-channel interference into useful signal power.

B. Region analysis

Converting the measurements to achievable spectral efficiency and selecting the best scheme at each position partitions the service area. This is the substantive conclusion: not that one scheme wins, but that the floor is zoned, and a practical multi-cell VLC system must know which zone a user occupies.

Which scheme wins depends on how throughput is counted. Compared link by link, JT wins at every position by construction, since two lamps driving one stream cannot be worse than one. The comparison that carries information counts throughput per cell: reuse and ZF serve two users simultaneously, while JT serves one and the others wait their turn. VII. Conclusion

VLC link-level key performance indicators depend strongly on where the user stands within the cell. In the two-cell setup, about 22% of the area near the cell centre operated reliably with simultaneous SISO transmissions under frequency reuse, around 45% in the inner cell edge required ZF precoding to handle moderate interference, and the remaining 33% near the outer cell edges required JT to cope with strong inter-cell interference.

Four points follow. The phosphor sets the ceiling: the 150–160 cm coverage radius is fixed by the slow phosphor conversion rather than by optics or noise, so blue filtering or micro-LEDs are the route past it. Transmitter-side linear precoding is sufficient, with no successive interference cancellation or receiver-side NOMA, which pushes complexity to the mains-powered and already-networked lighting controller. ZF and JT fail in opposite directions, the former to noise enhancement as the channel matrix degenerates and the latter to its single-user scheduling cost when the overlap is weak. Finally, position is the scheduling variable, and VLC supplies coarse localisation nearly for free because channel gain is a direct function of geometry.

Dividing the service area of a multi-cell VLC system into spatial regions and adapting the transmission scheme accordingly is therefore essential for reliable and efficient operation across user positions. The open question the split invites is handover: the boundaries move with mounting height, separation and detector field of view, so a deployment would need to learn its own zones rather than inherit these. Fig. 6 is the tool for that, and it is also where the model stops and the next experiment begins.
